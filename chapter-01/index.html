The Muscles of a Robot

How does a robot arm actually move? It's all about tiny, clever motors. In this chapter we'll open up the STS3215 servo that powers the LeRobot SO-101 arm — and learn the three big ideas that make robots work: torque, gear ratio, and degrees of freedom.

Part 1 · The Motor

Meet the STS3215 — a "smart" servo motor

Most toys use a plain motor that just spins when you give it power. The STS3215 is far cleverer. It's a serial bus servo: a motor, a gearbox, a sensor, and a tiny computer all packed into one little box.

Because it has a brain and a sensor, you can command it: "go to exactly 90.0°" — and it goes there and holds, correcting itself many times a second.

Why "serial bus"? You can daisy-chain all 6 motors on one wire. Each motor has an ID number (1–6), so a single cable can talk to every joint. That's why the SO-101 wiring is so tidy!
A real Feetech STS3215. Notice the brass output gear on top (that's what turns the joint) and the label with the model name.

What's inside? (four parts)

🔩 1. DC Motor

A small electric motor spins very fast but with very little force. Fast + weak — not useful for lifting yet.

⚙️ 2. Gearbox (1:345)

A stack of metal gears trades that speed for strength. The output turns 345× slower but 345× stronger. This is the gear ratio — Part 3!

🧲 3. Magnetic Encoder

A 12-bit magnetic sensor measures the exact angle. 12-bit = 4096 steps in one full turn — that's about 0.088° per step. Super precise!

🧠 4. Controller

A tiny chip reads the target angle, compares it to the sensor, and drives the motor to fix the error — up to ~1000× per second.

12VWorking voltage*
30kg·cmMax torque @ 12V*
4096Encoder steps / turn

*These are the specs of the STS3215 in the LeRobot Pro kit (Seeed Studio × Hugging Face): 30 kg·cm @ 12 V, a 12-bit magnetic encoder, and UART serial control. Feetech also makes a lighter 7.4 V / ~19 kg·cm version of the same STS3215 family.

Part 2 · The Motor Family

Other motors used in robots

A servo is just one tool. Engineers pick a motor to match the job. Here are the big families you'll meet in robotics:

SO-101 uses this

🤖 Smart Serial Servo (STS3215)

Knows its own angle, holds a position, talks over a data bus. Great for robot arm joints. Precise and easy to control.

🎮 Hobby RC Servo

The classic 3-wire servo in RC cars/planes. Moves within ~180° from a PWM signal but usually can't report its position back. Cheap and simple.

🔄 DC Motor

Just spins when powered — fast, cheap, strong-ish. No idea where it is. Perfect for wheels & fans, not precise joints.

👣 Stepper Motor

Moves in exact "steps" (clicks). Very accurate without a sensor. Powers 3D printers & CNC machines. Heavier and can lose its place if pushed too hard.

🚁 Brushless (BLDC)

Powerful, efficient, long-lasting. Runs drones, e-bikes and high-end robot legs. Needs a fancy electronic driver (ESC).

💪 What makes a servo special?

A servo = motor + gears + sensor + brain in one. It's "closed-loop": it constantly checks and corrects itself. That's exactly what a robot arm joint needs.

Part 3 · Big Idea #1

Torque — twisting strength

Torque is turning force. Not "how fast" — but "how hard can it twist?" It's what lets a joint lift the arm and hold a load without drooping.

Torque = Force × Distance from the pivot.

That's why a longer wrench loosens a stuck bolt more easily — more distance, more torque. Motors measure torque in kg·cm: how many kilograms it can hold at 1 cm out from the shaft.

Try it: Drag the load along the arm. Watch how the same weight needs much more torque when it's far from the motor — just like holding a bag close to your body vs. at arm's length. 💪

The Pro-kit STS3215 max torque ≈ 30 kg·cm (at 12 V). The dial shows how much of that "strength budget" your load uses.

Part 4 · Big Idea #2

Gear Ratio — trading speed for strength

A gear ratio is a deal: give up speed to gain torque (or the other way around). The little motor spins fast & weak; gears turn that into slow & strong.

Gear ratio (1 : N)1 : 345
×345Torque
÷345Speed
0.5Output RPM (approx)

The STS3215 in the SO-101 follower uses a 1:345 gearbox. The motor inside might spin ~180 RPM, but the joint turns only ~0.5 RPM — 345× stronger in exchange.

Cool fact — the leader arm is different! The arm you hold and wave around (the "leader") uses lighter gearing so it's easy to move by hand. The arm that does the work (the "follower") uses strong 1:345 gearing everywhere.
Leader jointGear ratio
Shoulder Pan / Elbow1 : 191
Shoulder Lift1 : 345 (strongest)
Wrist Flex / Roll / Gripper1 : 147 (quickest)

👉 Bigger number below the shoulder (which fights gravity most) = more strength where it's needed. That's engineering!

Part 5 · Deep Dive

From torque to work, power & energy

Torque is only half the story. To actually move a load a motor does work; how fast it works is power; and the fuel for all of it is energy. Four ideas, one neat chain. 🔗

The chain, in one line

torque × angle = Work = Energy used,   ÷ time = Power

Work is what happens when a torque turns through an angle: W = τ × θ. Do work and you've spent energy — same thing, same unit, the joule (J). Power is just how fast: P = W ÷ t.

The key motor formula: P = τ × ω — power equals torque times spin speed (ω, in rad/s). It's the twisting twin of “push × speed.”

And here's the payoff from Part 4: gears trade torque for speed but keep power about the same (τ×ω stays put). You never get free power — only a different mix of its two ingredients.

At the two ends, power is zero — no motion (stall) or no force (free spin) means no work gets done.

🔋 What if the arm ran on a battery instead of the wall?

The SO-101 normally plugs into the wall: an adapter turns AC mains into steady DC, and the energy is effectively unlimited — as long as it's plugged in.

A battery is different: it holds a fixed amount of energy. And because energy = power × time, that fixed tank sets how long the arm can run:

energy = capacity(Ah) × voltage(V)  →  Wh
run time = energy ÷ power   ( E = P × t )
It's the same E = P·t from the power curve above — just rearranged. Bigger battery = more energy = longer runtime; a hungrier motion (more watts) drains it faster.

That's why a fixed workshop arm uses the wall (endless energy, stable voltage), while a mobile robot carries a battery and must budget every joule. ⚖️

Part 6 · Big Idea #3

Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

A degree of freedom is one independent way a robot can move. Each motor in the SO-101 adds one DOF. More DOF = the robot can reach more poses and orientations.

Your own arm 💪

Point at something, then rotate your wrist while keeping your finger on it. You just used several degrees of freedom at once! Your shoulder alone has 3, your elbow 1, your wrist ~3.

The SO-101 has 6 motors → 6 degrees of freedom (5 to move the arm + 1 to open/close the gripper). Six is the magic number: it's enough to place the gripper at any position and any angle in its reach.

Rule of thumb: Count the motors that bend or twist the arm → that's your DOF. Wheels and the gripper's grip are usually counted separately.

Part 7 · Putting It Together

The SO-101 arm: 6 motors, 6 IDs

The real SO-101 follower arm — six STS3215 servos, numbered 1 (base) to 6 (gripper).

Every joint is one STS3215, each with a unique ID so the computer knows who's who on the shared cable. Here's the map from base to fingertip:

IDJointWhat it does
1shoulder_panRotates whole arm left/right
2shoulder_liftRaises/lowers the arm

4wrist_flexTilts the wrist up/down

In LeRobot's code the joints are listed in exactly this order: shoulder_pan, shoulder_lift, elbow_flex, wrist_flex, wrist_roll, gripper — each a Motor("sts3215").

Wrap-up

What you learned 🎓

Torque

Twisting strength = force × distance. The Pro-kit STS3215 gives 30 kg·cm @ 12V.

Gear Ratio

Trade speed for strength. SO-101 follower = 1:345 everywhere.

Degrees of Freedom

One motor = one DOF. The SO-101 has 6.

🔎 Bonus: how does the motor "hold" a position?

It runs a tiny loop hundreds of times a second: read the encoder → compare to the target → if there's an error, push the motor to shrink it. This is called closed-loop control (a PID controller). It's the same idea as balancing on a bike: sense, correct, repeat — very fast.

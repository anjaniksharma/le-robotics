Part 2 · Seeing

How a network sees (ResNet, briefly)

A photo is just a grid of numbers — each pixel's brightness. Early layers of a vision network learn to spot tiny edges; later layers combine edges into shapes, then whole objects. Stack enough layers and it can recognize a mug, a cube, a hand.

ResNet is a famous, very deep vision network. Its trick was adding “shortcut” connections that let information skip ahead — which made training very deep networks actually work. We don't need its guts; just remember: ResNet turns an image into a compact list of “what's in it” numbers. The robot will use exactly this to look through its camera. 📷

Part 3 · Words as Numbers

Predicting the next token

To a language model, text is a sequence of tokens — little chunks (a word or word-piece). The model reads the tokens so far and outputs a probability for every possible next token. That's it — that's just classification, like Chapter 4, but with a huge number of categories (the whole vocabulary).

P(next token | the tokens so far) a probability for every word in the vocabulary — they sum to 1

The magical part: the training data labels itself. Take any sentence; at each position the “correct answer” is literally the word that actually came next. Feed the model billions of sentences and it learns which words tend to follow which. Loss = the same “surprise” (cross-entropy) from Chapter 4.

Self-supervised: the answer is in the text

The model sees the words up to a point and must guess the next one:

therobotpicksupthe?

Correct next token here → cube. If the model gave “cube” a high probability, tiny loss. If it was sure it'd be “banana,” big loss — and backprop nudges the weights.

No human labelled anything — the text is its own answer key. That's why it's called self-supervised.

Part 5 · One Line on Architecture

What's a Transformer?

We promised to keep architecture short, so here it is in one idea: a Transformer uses attention — a way for each word to look back at the other words and decide which ones matter for predicting what's next.

In “the robot picks up the ___”, attention lets “picks up” and “robot” shout loudest, steering the guess toward something grippable. That's the whole superpower: every token can weigh every other token. Everything else is plumbing.

The same Transformer idea — attention over a sequence — is what the robot's ACT brain uses too. Onward to the robot. 🤖

Part 6 · The Robot's Brain

ACT — predicting motions instead of words

Meet ACT (Action Chunking Transformer), the policy you train in LeRobot to make the SO-101 act on its own. It's the same machine we've been building — with a camera for eyes and motors for a mouth.

How ACT is wired (lightly)

  • Input — sees & feels: the camera image goes through a ResNet (Part 2), and it also reads the 6 joint numbers (the calibrated encoders from Chapter 3).
  • Brain: a Transformer (Part 5) mixes those together.
  • Output — acts: instead of the next word, it predicts the next motions — a whole chunk of future actions, each action = the 6 target motor positions.
loss = | predicted motion − your actual motion | L1 — the exact regression loss from Chapter 4

Where does the training data come from? From you. Every time you teleoperated and recorded (Chapters 2–3, lerobot-record), you saved thousands of moments of camera + joints → the move you made. ACT learns to imitate you.

Text vs robot, side by side: GPT classifies the next token (pick one word → cross-entropy). ACT regresses the next motions (predict continuous numbers → L1). Same loop, different output — that's the whole connection. 🎯

🔎 Why predict a whole “chunk” of actions at once?

Predicting one tiny step at a time can get jittery and can drift off course. ACT predicts a short chunk — roughly the next second of motion — so the movement is smoother and more committed. It executes a few, looks again, and predicts the next chunk. (The "Chunking" in Action Chunking Transformer.)

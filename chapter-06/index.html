Collecting Good Data

In Chapter 5 we learned the robot's brain (ACT) learns by copying your demonstrations. So here's the catch: it copies everything — your smooth moves and your shaky camera, your good lighting and your bad. The policy can never be better than the data. This chapter is about being a great teacher: how to set up the scene, the cameras, and the episodes so the robot can actually learn.

Part 1 · The Golden Rule

Garbage in, garbage out

ACT is trained by imitation: you show it how, it copies you. That makes you the teacher and your recordings the entire textbook. A brilliant learning algorithm still can't learn a good policy from sloppy demonstrations.

Great teacher →

Steady camera, even lighting, the cube in different spots each time, smooth confident moves, plenty of examples. → a robot that works in the real, messy world.

Careless teacher →

Camera bumped between takes, flickering light, cube always in the same spot, hesitant jerky moves, only a handful of examples. → a robot that fails the moment anything changes.

Everything in this chapter serves one goal: make your recordings consistent where the robot needs reliability, and varied where it needs to generalize. That balance is the whole art. ⚖️
Part 2 · The Atom of Data

What one frame actually is

A recording is a stack of frames. Each frame is a single synchronized snapshot — and it's exactly the input→output pair ACT learns from (Chapter 5):

  • observation.images.front — the camera picture(s) 📷
  • observation.state — the 6 current joint numbers (calibrated, Ch3)
  • action — the 6 target numbers (what you did next)
  • timestamp · frame_index · episode_index · task

Record 30 of these every second, for a minute, across 50 tries, and you have a dataset. Everything else in this chapter is about making each snapshot count.

Part 3 · Eyes

Cameras: position & resolution

The robot only knows what its cameras show. Two rules matter most:

  • Placement. A front/top camera sees the whole workspace; a wrist camera (on the arm) gives a close-up that follows the gripper. Two views beat one — when the arm blocks the front view, the wrist camera still sees the object.
  • Keep it still & consistent. The camera must not move between episodes, and must be in the same place when the robot runs on its own. Bump it, and every learned landmark shifts.
  • Resolution. LeRobot's default is 640×480. Higher = more detail but slower & bigger; lower = tiny and blurry. Whatever you pick, use the same at record and deploy time.
Try it → Switch cameras and drop the resolution. Watch the wrist cam stay locked on the cube while the front cam loses it behind the arm — and see detail vanish as pixels shrink.

Part 4 · Timing

Frames per second

FPS is how many snapshots you take each second. LeRobot records at fps = 30 by default. More fps captures fast motion more smoothly, but makes bigger files and demands more from your computer.

Critically, your machine must keep up. If the record loop runs slower than the target fps, LeRobot warns that frames get dropped — leaving gaps in the motion the robot learns. A dataset is a clock ticking at a steady fps; every tick must land.

frames in an episode = fps × episode seconds
30 fps × 30 s ≈ 900 frames per episode
Slide the fps → Watch the same reaching motion sampled coarsely vs finely. Low fps = choppy, missing detail; high fps = smooth.

Part 5 · Storage

How images are encoded

A picture is just a grid of pixels, and each pixel is three numbers (red, green, blue). One 640×480 frame is ~920,000 numbers. At 30 fps that's a flood of data — far too much to store raw.

So LeRobot saves the camera streams as compressed MP4 video (with a codec like h264 or libsvtav1) instead of millions of loose images. Compression throws away what your eye won't miss, shrinking the data 10–100×. The joint numbers stay as exact values in .parquet tables.

Why it matters for you: more cameras × higher resolution × higher fps × more/longer episodes = a much bigger dataset and slower training. Pick what the task needs, not the maximum.

Part 6 · The Scene

Lighting, background & the object

The robot keys on whatever is reliably in the picture. Your job: keep the helpful things steady and vary the right things on purpose.

💡 Lighting

Bright and even. Avoid flicker, glare, and moving shadows. If the light changes between recording and running, a policy that secretly relied on a shadow will fail.

🖼️ Background

Clean and uncluttered so the object stands out — but not a perfectly bare void. A little natural clutter that stays put is fine; random new objects are distractions.

🧊 The object

Same object, but move its starting position every episode. Otherwise the robot memorizes "reach to that exact spot" instead of learning to find the object.

The consistency ↔ variety balance: hold the camera, lighting, and background steady (reliability); deliberately vary the object position, and your grasp approach a little (robustness). Get this right and the policy works when the world isn't perfect.
Part 7 · Episodes

Arm setup & episode length

An episode is one complete attempt at the task — approach, grasp, move, place, done. A few rules of thumb:

  • Start from the same home pose each episode, so the robot always begins from familiar ground.
  • Keep episodes tight. LeRobot's episode_time_s caps each one (default 60 s; many SO-101 tasks are 10–30 s). Don't record long fumbling — clean, purposeful attempts teach best.
  • Reset between episodes. reset_time_s gives you time to reposition the cube and re-home the arm.
  • Record many. Diversity across dozens of episodes (num_episodes, often 25–100+) is what makes a policy robust.

Part 8 · The Result

How the collected data looks on disk

Recording produces a LeRobotDataset — plain, tool-free files anyone can open:

my_dataset/
├─ data/ # joint states & actions
│ └─ *.parquet
├─ videos/ # one MP4 per camera
│ ├─ observation.images.front/*.mp4
│ └─ observation.images.wrist/*.mp4
└─ meta/ # info.json, tasks, stats

The .parquet tables hold the exact numbers (states, actions, indices, timestamps); the .mp4 files hold the synchronized camera frames. That's the whole dataset. 🎉

Wrap-up

Your good-data checklist ✅

Keep steady (reliability)

  • Camera fixed & identical at record + deploy
  • Even, flicker-free lighting
  • Same home start pose each episode
  • Same resolution & fps throughout

Vary on purpose (robustness)

  • Object start position, every episode
  • Small differences in your approach/grasp
  • Enough episodes (dozens+)
  • Smooth, confident, purposeful demos

The command that records it all (from Chapter 2's port, Chapter 3's calibration):

lerobot-record --robot.type=so101_follower --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodemXXXX --robot.id=my_follower_arm \ --robot.cameras="{front:{type:opencv,index_or_path:0,width:640,height:480,fps:30}, wrist:{type:opencv,index_or_path:1,width:640,height:480,fps:30}}" \ --teleop.type=so101_leader --teleop.port=/dev/tty.usbmodemYYYY --teleop.id=my_leader_arm \ --dataset.repo_id=me/grab_cube --dataset.single_task="Grab the cube" --dataset.num_episodes=40 --dataset.fps=30 --dataset.episode_time_s=20

Part 1 · The Idea, At Scale

Training = the Chapter-5 loop, 100,000 times

Nothing new happens in training — it's the exact loop from Chapter 5, now run over your recorded dataset:

  1. Grab a batch of recorded frames (camera + joints).
  2. ACT predicts the action chunk for each.
  3. Compare to what you actually did → the L1 loss (Chapters 4 & 5).
  4. Backprop → nudge every weight a little.
  5. Repeat ~100,000 times.
loss = | ACT's predicted motion − your recorded motion | averaged over the batch — exactly the regression loss from Chapter 5

Each pass makes ACT copy you a tiny bit better. Do it enough times and the network can generalize the pattern to new situations it never saw.

Part 2 · Vocabulary

Steps, batches & epochs

Batch

A handful of frames processed together before one weight update. LeRobot's default is batch_size = 8.

Step

One batch → one nudge of the weights. ACT trains for steps = 100,000 by default.

Epoch

One full pass over your whole dataset. Big datasets = many steps per epoch; small ones repeat often.

Assumes batch size 8. The same frames get revisited many times — that repetition is how the pattern sinks in.

The network sees each frame many times over a training run — like reviewing the same flashcards until the pattern is second nature. Too few steps and it hasn't learned; the graph on the next slide tells you when to stop. 📉

On a good GPU, 100k steps for ACT takes a few hours. On a laptop CPU it could take days — which is exactly why we'll borrow a cloud GPU soon. ☁️

Two things to feel: a tiny learning rate barely moves; a huge one makes the loss explode. And with too few episodes, the training loss keeps dropping while real-world performance gets worse — that's overfitting (memorizing instead of learning, straight from Chapter 7).
Part 4 · Save Points

Checkpoints

Training for hours is risky — what if it crashes at step 90,000? So LeRobot saves the model to disk every save_freq = 20,000 steps (and at the end), into output_dir/checkpoints/.

Checkpoints are like save points in a video game:

  • Resume a crashed run with --resume=true instead of starting over.
  • Pick the best one — a later checkpoint isn't always better (see: overfitting).
  • Deploy any checkpoint by pointing --policy.path at it.
Click a save point → load different checkpoints and see how far along that version of the brain is.

☁️ Sub-section: Training in the cloud (Colab & Hugging Face)

Here's the beautiful part: your Mac never needs a GPU. The Hugging Face Hub is the bridge — you push the dataset up, a cloud GPU trains on it, and you pull the finished policy back down to run on the arm.

💻 Mac
record + push_to_hub

🤗 HF Hub
your dataset

☁️ Cloud GPU

🤗 HF Hub

→
💻 Mac
rollout on arm

Google Colab gives you a free GPU in a browser notebook — great for a first training run. The flow:

  1. Record locally and let it upload: --dataset.push_to_hub=true (Chapter 7).
  2. Open LeRobot's ACT training notebook in Colab, switch the runtime to a GPU, and sign in with hf auth login.
  3. Run lerobot-train there — it pulls your dataset from the Hub and pushes the trained policy back.
# inside the Colab GPU notebook lerobot-train --dataset.repo_id=$HF_USER/grab_cube --policy.type=act \ --output_dir=outputs/train/act_grab_cube --policy.device=cuda \ --policy.repo_id=$HF_USER/act_grab_cube --wandb.enable=true

Hugging Face Jobs runs training on a managed GPU with one extra flag — no notebook, no setup. Add --job.target=<gpu> to the same command and it dispatches to the cloud.

# run hf auth login first; list GPUs with: hf jobs hardware lerobot-train --dataset.repo_id=$HF_USER/grab_cube --policy.type=act \ --policy.repo_id=$HF_USER/act_grab_cube \ --job.target=h200 --job.timeout=8h

The pod trains on the Hub dataset and pushes the policy to your account. --job.detach submits and exits; hf jobs hardware shows the flavors & pricing.

Why the Hub matters: it decouples where you record (your Mac + arm) from where you train (a big GPU). Record here, train anywhere, run here. That's the whole cloud workflow. 🌉
Part 6 · Exam Day

🤖 Run the policy on the arm

Training done, policy in hand — now the robot performs alone. Deployment runs a tight loop: look → predict a chunk → move → look again. The command for this is lerobot-rollout (record was for data only).

Press Run and watch the trained policy attempt the grasp on its own.

The autonomous loop

📷 observe→🧠 ACT→🎯 action chunk→⚙️ motors🔁

It executes a few actions from the chunk, looks again, predicts the next chunk, and repeats — the same autoregression that writes text (Chapter 6), but for motion.

lerobot-rollout --policy.path=$HF_USER/act_grab_cube \ --robot.type=so101_follower --robot.port=/dev/tty.usbmodemXXXX \ --robot.cameras="{front:{type:opencv,index_or_path:0,width:640,height:480,fps:30}}" \ --task="Grab the cube" --duration=60
Flip the mismatch switch: if the camera or lighting at deploy time differs from training, the policy misses — the exact lesson from Chapter 7. Your data setup is your deploy setup.
Part 7 · When It Misbehaves

Reading the failures

A misbehaving policy is usually a data or training problem, not bad luck. The fix almost always points back to earlier chapters:

SymptomLikely causeFix
🤪 Jittery, shaky motionJerky demos, or undertrainedSmoother demos (Ch7); train longer
🎯 Reaches the wrong spotCamera moved since trainingPut the camera back exactly (Ch7)
😴 Barely moves / gives upToo few episodes; undertrainedRecord more; more steps
🏠 Works on the bench, fails elsewhereOverfit — no variety in dataVary object position & lighting (Ch7)
💥 Loss never droppedLearning rate too high; bad labelsLower LR; check the task/data
See the pattern? Every deploy failure traces back to data quality or how long/how well you trained. Great robots are built by great teachers with patience. 🧑‍🏫
